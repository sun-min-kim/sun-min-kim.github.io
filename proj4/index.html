A.2: Recover Homographies

A homography is a 3x3 matrix that maps points in one image to another. Each pair of matching points gives 2 equations to match points between images. In the image examples I had, a selection of 8 pairs of points was used.

When picking the 8 points, I chose sharp corners of objects (like windows, fences, and cars), since corners are easier to match at the same spot in both images.

For the systems of equations, I used the matrix equation A • h = b, and then the following two rows are used to calculate the corresponding points:

[ x   y   1   0   0   0   -x·x'   -y·x' ] · h = x'
[ 0   0   0   x   y   1   -x·y'   -y·y' ] · h = y'

h = [ a  b  c  d  e  f  g  h ]ᵀ        H = [ a  b  c ]
                                           [ d  e  f ]
                                           [ g  h  1 ]

where (x, y) is the point in the first image and (x', y') is the point in the second image. By stacking these 8 points, we get 16 equations total with 8 unknowns:

[ x1  y1  1   0   0   0   -x1·x1'  -y1·x1' ]         [ x1' ]
[ 0   0   0   x1  y1  1   -x1·y1'  -y1·y1' ]         [ y1' ]
[                   ...                    ] · h  =  [ ... ]
[ x8  y8  1   0   0   0   -x8·x8'  -y8·x8' ]         [ x8' ]
[ 0   0   0   x8  y8  1   -x8·y8'  -y8·y8' ]         [ y8' ]

I then calculated the values using np.linalg.lstsq.

A.3: Warp the Images

To warp the images, I used the image that was going to be warped, and for each pixel used the inverse of H to find where that pixel would map to in the original image. When doing this, there are two methods that can be used: Nearest Neighbor copies the closest pixel by rounding the coordinate values, Bilinear blends the 4 pixels around the spot to get what the pixel should look like.

Rectification

In the below two examples, I rectified the storefront window in the first image of Bancroft, and the metal fence on the second floor of the apartment photo. I clicked the 4 corners of the rectangles as they appeared in the image, which were then mapped to a straight rectangle. For each of the rectangles, I chose a pixel size of (440 x 200) and (500 x 200) respectively, which I estimated from the photos. As a result of rectifying, the rectangles selected become straight and sharp, warping the perspective of the photo so that it looks like we are looking at the selected rectangles head on.

Nearest Neighbor vs. Bilinear Interpolation

Zoomed in on the "Right Only" sign

The two photos show warping the image by using the Nearest Neighbor and Bilinear approaches. As a whole, the two photos look effectively the same, particularly when it comes to how much the image stretches in which dimensions. However, there are slight differences when you zoom into diagonal lines in the image. For instance, zooming into the Right Only sign in the Nearest Neighbor photo shows jagged edges on the photo as the pixels are taken from the nearest one. The Bilinear photo, however, doesn't show this and has a lot smoother edges, as a result of blending the 4 nearest pixels instead of rounding. As a result, I believe the Bilinear approach is better in terms of image quality. However, it does take longer to run on average than Nearest Neighbor, averaging about 2~3 times more processing time for the same image (Nearest Neighbor: 0.368s, Bilinear: 0.912s).

For the rest of the mosaic images made, I made use of the Bilinear approach as the image quality was slightly better, despite the tradeoff in time.

A.4: Blend the Images into a Mosaic

Using everything done in the previous sections, mosaics of the images can be made, merging the 3 images together. For all of these, the middle image was used as the center reference, and the left and right images were warped to match its proportions. In order to do this, the right image's point pairs were effectively swapped to get the right side direction. Using these warped images, I made one larger image that shows all of the images combined using each image's offset. For blending, I made use of distance_transform_edt, where each pixel's weight is its distance to the edge of the image, which helped make the transition between images more seamless.

Mosaic 1: Downtown Bancroft (Horizontal)

This mosaic came out clean as a whole, with the edges blending in well and the street looking consistent across the image, especially if the black bars at the top and bottom are cropped off. One weakness though is that the person walking in the left image's distortion is more noticeable; however, this is to be expected as the image itself is being warped, and disproportionate humans are naturally more noticeable to us. Overall though, the street and building look clean and smooth.

Mosaic 2: Downtown Bancroft (Vertical)

This mosaic came out similarly to the previous horizontal Bancroft mosaic. However, one noticeable difference is the area where the middle and right images merge. There is a bit of blur and unfocusedness, likely due to a non-perfect alignment of points between the two images. However, ignoring this discrepancy, the mosaic as a whole looks good.

Mosaic 3: Apartment

For this last mosaic of the apartment, I made use of a mix of horizontal and vertical photos to capture different areas of the apartment. As a result, the black ribbon shape on the top and the bottom of the mosaic is more pronounced, compared to the other mosaics as there is a bigger difference in height between the middle image to the left and right images. However, the mosaic itself is clean, seamlessly blending together the three different photos of my apartment to create a simulated wide angle photo.

B.1: Harris Corners and ANMS

In the image below, I ran Harris on a grayscaled image of my apartment to find all of the corners. I then kept only the corners that were at least 10% the strength of the max Harris score seen in the image. In the case of the apartment example below, this cut down the total number of corners detected from 84,771 to 2,187. I then used ANMS (Adaptive Non-Maximal Suppression) to pick the 500 best spread corners. To do this, each corner's radius was set to the distance to the nearest corner whose score times 0.9 was still greater than its own score. By doing this, the corners became more spread out and less cluttered as can be seen in the plots below.

B.2: Feature Descriptors

In order to get feature descriptors for each of the images, I made use of the Gaussian Kernel to blur the image first. Then for each corner detected, a 40x40 window was cut out which was shrunk down to an 8x8 patch (which was done by taking every 5th pixel). Since we used the gaussian kernel earlier to blur the image already, every 5th pixel represented the average of the pixels around it. I then normalized the patch by subtracting its mean from it, and then dividing by its standard deviation. Below is some of the feature descriptors represented as 8x8 patches. Most of them show the corners through a sharp shift in color, which can be seen as changes from yellow/green pixels to dark navy pixels.

B.3: Feature Matching

For matching features between two images, I used a threshold ratio of 0.5 between the first and second nearest neighbors distance. This resulted in less than 50 matches (38 matches in the example shown below) where all of the feature matches were accurate as far as the human eye (my eye) could tell.

B.4: RANSAC and Automatic Mosaics

Below are the mosaics created automatically using RANSAC and parts of code reused from Part A. I made use of 4 point RANSAC which did the following operations: selected 4 random matches, computed the Homography, counted how many matches were within 5 pixels, repeated 2000 times, fit the Homography to the best matches found. One thing I noticed about the automatic mosaics is that the mosaics were a lot more sharp on the edges where the images were merged. An example can be seen in the Vertical Downtown Bancroft example where the red pickup truck in the manual mosaic is blurred, but sharp in the automatic mosaic. The pickup truck is located on the seam between two images and was used for some of the points I selected when creating the manual mosaic. This made it more prone to human error as selecting the points manually could have slight inaccuracies, like selecting slightly different features of the pickup truck to match or simply slightly misclicking, making the feature matches a bit more inaccurate, resulting in the blur. One thing that the automatic mosaics couldn't fix though were movement in between images as well as disfiguring in the non-central images, as can be seen with the pedestrian in the Horizontal Downtown Bancroft mosaic. This makes sense though since the process of transforming the image itself to form the mosaic is the same process between the manual and automatic mosaics.
